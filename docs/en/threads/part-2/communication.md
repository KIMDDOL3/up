# Communication: Let Understanding Come Before Winning

When a conversation matters, we often speak fastest at the moment we understand least. We defend an intention before hearing an impact, turn a request into a test of love, or use a familiar label to avoid naming what actually happened.

This chapter draws on the practice commonly called nonviolent communication. Its purpose is not to make every conversation pleasant or to persuade another person to agree. It is a way to make experience, responsibility, and choice more visible: what happened, what moved in us, what matters, and what we are asking for. It also leaves room for a clear no, a firm boundary, outside help, and leaving when a relationship is unsafe.

The four steps are a language for taking responsibility for our side of a conversation. They are not a script for controlling someone else's response.

## Quick Overview

- separate observable facts from conclusions about a person's character;
- name feelings and the human needs beneath them without assigning blame;
- make a concrete request that leaves room for a real no;
- listen for understanding before offering advice or a solution;
- pause when capacity is low, and put safety before communication technique;
- let apology face impact and let repair show up as sustained action;
- practise one low-risk conversation before carrying the framework into a difficult relationship.

## 1. Communication Is Not Winning

The aim of a difficult conversation is not to prove that one person is right. It is to make the situation more visible: what happened, what each person experienced, what matters, and what choices remain. Clear communication does not remove judgment, responsibility, or boundaries. It gives them a more honest path.

Before speaking, ask: What is the one concrete event I want the other person to understand? What did I feel, and what mattered underneath it? What am I asking for, and what will I do if the answer is no? If you cannot answer yet, say that you need a short pause and return when you can separate fact from story.

## 2. The Four Steps

### Observation: describe what a camera could record

An observation is a concrete event, quotation, number, or change. It is different from a conclusion about a person's character.

- Observation: “The document changed after I approved version three, and I did not receive a message.”
- Evaluation: “You always undermine me.”

The second sentence may express real pain, but it gives the other person a label to dispute. Start with the part that can be checked. If you are uncertain, say so: “I may have missed a message. I can see the shared file changed at 4:10, and I did not understand whether the release was still planned.”

### Feeling: name an emotion, not a verdict

Feelings tell us how an event is affecting us. Words such as sad, afraid, relieved, lonely, frustrated, grateful, and hopeful point to an inner experience. Some sentences use “I feel” to introduce a judgment:

- Feeling: “I feel anxious and disappointed.”
- Verdict disguised as feeling: “I feel ignored and manipulated.”

The second sentence may describe how the situation seems, but “ignored” and “manipulated” also assign an intention. You can make both parts visible: “I feel hurt, and I am telling myself that my concern did not matter to you. Is that what you intended?”

### Need: identify the value beneath the strategy

A need is a human value or condition that matters: trust, rest, clarity, fairness, autonomy, safety, belonging, consideration, or play. A strategy is one possible way to meet it.

- Need: “I need predictability before I commit my evening.”
- Strategy presented as a need: “I need you to text me every five minutes.”

Naming the need opens more than one path. You may still request a particular action, but you will know what problem the action is meant to solve.

### Request: make a doable invitation

A request is specific, present-tense, and possible to accept or decline. It names an action, a time, and sometimes a place or standard. “Be more considerate” describes a hope; “Would you send me a message by 6:30 if you expect to be late?” gives the other person something they can answer.

A request becomes a demand when a no will be punished, mocked, or treated as proof of disloyalty. The same words can therefore be a request in one relationship and coercion in another. Leave space for a different proposal, a later answer, or a boundary you can carry out yourself.

Put together, the four steps can sound like this:

```text
When I observe [specific fact], I feel [emotion].
I need [value or condition].
Would you be willing to [one concrete action] by [time]?
If not, I will [a boundary or next step I can carry out].
```

The final line is not a threat. It keeps responsibility with the speaker. It also prevents a communication model from becoming a polite way to wait forever for someone else to change.

## 3. Three Everyday Translations

The examples below are not perfect sentences. They show how to move from an accusation to a conversation that leaves both people with more information and more choice.

### A partner arrives late

Reactive version:

> “You do not care about me. You are always late.”

Four-step version:

> “We agreed to meet at seven, and you arrived at 7:40 without a message. I felt worried and then resentful because I need reliability and a chance to plan my evening. Would you text me by 7:10 when you know you will be late? If you cannot do that tonight, I will eat first and reschedule rather than wait without a plan.”

The request does not require the partner to agree that they are a bad person. It gives them a clear action and gives the speaker a limit that does not depend on winning an argument.

### A family member pressures a choice

Reactive version:

> “You never respect my decisions. Stop interfering in my life.”

Four-step version:

> “You have asked three times this week whether I will take that job, and you sent the listing to two relatives. I feel tense and exposed because I need privacy and room to decide. Would you ask me once a week and keep our conversation private until I have decided? If the questions continue today, I will end the conversation and return to it tomorrow.”

This version does not deny the family member's concern. It separates care from permission to manage another adult's decision.

### A colleague changes a project late

Reactive version:

> “You are impossible to work with. You keep changing everything at the last minute.”

Four-step version:

> “The acceptance criteria changed in the document at 4 p.m., after I had completed the test run. I feel frustrated and uncertain because I need a stable target and enough time to check the result. Would you mark changes in the shared document and confirm the owner before 2 p.m. tomorrow? If the criteria are still unconfirmed, I will pause the release and record the risk for the team.”

The speaker names a work consequence rather than attacking a personality. The team can now discuss timing, ownership, and risk even if the request needs to change.

In each example, the other person may still say no. Clear language increases the chance of understanding; it does not guarantee cooperation.

## 4. Self-Empathy Before the Next Sentence

When we are flooded with anger, fear, or shame, a carefully formatted request can still carry the force of an accusation. Before asking another person to listen, give yourself a short place to land.

1. **Pause the story.** Put down the phone or delay the reply if you can do so safely. Notice breathing, tension, heat, fatigue, and the urge to attack or disappear.
2. **Separate event from interpretation.** Write one sentence that a recording could support, then write the meaning your mind added. Both deserve attention, but they are not the same fact.
3. **Name the feeling.** Choose a few ordinary words. You do not need to prove that the feeling is reasonable before acknowledging it.
4. **Ask what matters.** Is the unmet need safety, rest, respect, clarity, choice, repair, or something else? A need is not an accusation against the person who failed to meet it.
5. **Choose one next action.** It may be a request, a boundary, a pause, an apology, or support from someone outside the conflict.

Self-empathy is not self-excuse. “I was frightened” can explain a reaction without making shouting harmless. It is also not self-erasure. You can recognise another person's pain and still protect your own boundary.

A private note might read:

> “The message said the plan was cancelled after I had already travelled there. I feel embarrassed and angry. I need consideration and reliable information. I will wait until I can speak without insulting anyone, then ask for a clear notice rule. If this keeps happening, I will stop accepting last-minute arrangements.”

The note may never be sent. Its first job is to help you stop handing an unprocessed alarm to the next person.

## 5. Listening Without Taking Over

Listening is not waiting silently until it is your turn to correct the record. It is a temporary decision to help another person hear their own experience more clearly.

Try a small sequence:

1. **Ask permission:** “Do you want listening, questions, or help thinking through options?”
2. **Reflect what you heard:** “You expected the change to be discussed before the meeting, and you felt alone when it was announced.”
3. **Check, rather than diagnose:** “Did I get that right?” or “Are you feeling disappointed, or is another word closer?”
4. **Ask about the need:** “What matters most to you now: acknowledgement, information, rest, or a plan?”
5. **Respect correction:** If the person says you misunderstood, do not defend the first guess. Try again or ask what they want you to understand.

Empathy does not require agreement with every interpretation. You can say, “I understand why that felt frightening,” without claiming that every conclusion is accurate. If facts remain disputed, return to them after the person has been heard.

Your capacity is part of honest listening. “I want to hear this, and I have ten minutes before I need to sleep. Shall we continue tomorrow?” is kinder than promising unlimited attention and becoming resentful.

## 6. A Request Must Leave Room for No

A relationship becomes safer when a request can be declined without retaliation. The answer “no” may still disappoint you; it does not automatically mean the other person does not love, respect, or value you.

When someone declines, try:

> “I hear that you cannot do that. Is there another option you are willing to consider, or should I make a different plan?”

When you decline, offer only what is true:

> “I cannot take this on tonight. I can look at it tomorrow morning, or I can help you find another person.”

Do not add a false yes to prevent an uncomfortable moment. A clear no lets the other person plan; a reluctant yes often returns later as resentment or withdrawal.

If a request is important enough to have a consequence, say the consequence as your own action:

- Request: “Would you keep our financial records in the shared folder?”
- Boundary: “If the records are not available by Friday, I will not approve another shared expense until we can review them.”
- Control: “You are forbidden to spend any money unless I approve it.”

The difference is not whether the speaker has a preference. It is whether the other person's choice is met with information and a boundary, or with punishment and forced obedience.

## 7. Communication Has a Safety Boundary

Nonviolent communication is not a treatment for violence, threats, stalking, coercive control, or a power arrangement in which refusal brings danger. Someone who is afraid may not be able to speak freely, negotiate calmly, or offer the other person emotional care.

When safety is in doubt:

- move to a safer place if you can;
- contact a trusted person, local crisis service, domestic-violence service, or emergency support as appropriate;
- protect essential documents, money, medication, communication, and a way to leave;
- avoid a private “final conversation” when it could increase danger;
- use professional support for risk planning rather than trying to solve the situation with better wording.

Power differences matter even without visible violence. A manager, caregiver, parent, landlord, or financial provider may be able to make a refusal costly. In those settings, offer private ways to decline, clear information, a cooling-off period, an independent support person, and a real exit route. Do not call silence consent when the person cannot safely say no.

After safety is established, communication skills may help with coordination or repair. They should never be used to place responsibility for another person's violence on the person being harmed.

## 8. Apology and Repair

Communication also matters after we have caused harm. An apology should name the specific action, acknowledge its impact, ask what the other person needs, offer an observable correction, and allow distance or no forgiveness. “I am sorry you felt hurt” hides responsibility; “I shared your private story without checking, and that took away your choice about who knew it” names the action and the impact.

Repair is tested over time. A long explanation cannot cancel repeated harm. Change appears in the next decision, the next conflict, and the next moment involving privacy or consent. Reconciliation is not the acceptance test for a sincere apology: someone can recognise changed behaviour and still choose distance.

## 9. Seven Days of Practice

Use one low-risk conversation. Do not choose the most dangerous relationship as your first exercise.

1. **Day 1:** write one recurring conflict without judging either person's character; underline the facts a camera could record.
2. **Day 2:** list the feelings that arise and the needs or values beneath them.
3. **Day 3:** turn one strategy into a small, concrete request with a time and place.
4. **Day 4:** write the boundary you will carry out if the request is declined or the pattern repeats.
5. **Day 5:** listen for ten minutes, reflect what you heard, and ask whether your understanding is accurate.
6. **Day 6:** practise receiving a no without punishment, or practise giving an honest no with one alternative you can truly offer.
7. **Day 7:** review what changed. Record one repair action, one unresolved fact, and the next date to revisit the conversation.

Do not use the exercise to make someone participate. Your part is the clarity of your observation, the honesty of your feeling, the ownership of your need, the freedom in your request, and the boundary you can keep. ## Closing: Understanding Is an Invitation

Good communication is not the art of finding a sentence that makes another person behave. It is the practice of telling the truth without turning a person into a problem, hearing pain without surrendering judgment, and keeping choice alive on both sides.

Some conversations will lead to repair. Some will reveal a boundary, a need for outside support, or the end of a relationship. In every case, we can try to leave fewer hidden accusations behind: one observable fact, one honest feeling, one human need, and one request that the other person is free to answer.
